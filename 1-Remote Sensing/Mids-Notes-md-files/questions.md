## Section A: From Lecture 1 (Remote Sensing Overview)

---

### Q1. What is Remote Sensing?
**Ans:** Remote sensing is the acquisition of information about an object or phenomenon **without being in physical contact** with it.

---

### Q2. What is Modelling (in RS context)?
**Ans:** Modelling is a process of describing a system through **precise mathematical relations** of inputs and outputs to simulate its present, past, or future behavior.

---

### Q3. What are the four functions of Remote Sensing? (The 4 M's)
**Ans:** Mapping, Measuring, Modelling, and Monitoring.

---

### Q4. What is the difference between Data and Information?
**Ans:** **Data** is raw representation that can be operated upon. **Information** is data that has been interpreted by human beings.

---

### Q5. Name three geospatial data sources.
**Ans:** (Any three of)
1. Field/Ground Surveys
2. Photography (Ground and Aerial)
3. Remote Sensing (Space and Airborne imaging)
4. Positioning Systems (GPS)

---

### Q6. List the 7 steps of the Remote Sensing process.
**Ans:**
1. Energy Source/Illumination
2. Radiation and the Atmosphere
3. Interaction with the Target
4. Recording by the Sensor
5. Transmission, Reception, and Processing
6. Interpretation and Analysis
7. Application

---

### Q7. What is a Remote Sensor?
**Ans:** A detection and recording instrument that collects information about objects **without physical contact**, carried on aircraft or spacecraft.

---

### Q8. Name any three advantages of Remote Sensing.
**Ans:**
1. Large area coverage
2. Repetitive coverage for monitoring dynamic features
3. Data acquisition over inaccessible areas

---

### Q9. Name any three limitations of Remote Sensing.
**Ans:**
1. Often oversold — cannot provide all needed information
2. Human interaction may introduce errors
3. RS data can be expensive to collect

---

### Q10. What is Earth Resource Information?
**Ans:** Any information concerning vegetation, soils, minerals, rocks, water, atmospheric characteristics, and urban infrastructure.

---

### Q11. What does "Eye in the sky" refer to?
**Ans:** Remote sensors carried on aircraft and Earth-orbiting spacecraft.

---

### Q12. What are the three components of an Earth Observation System?
**Ans:** Scene (Earth surface + atmosphere), RS Systems (sensors + platforms), and Processing/Analysis.

---

### Q13. What was TIROS-1?
**Ans:** The first weather satellite (1960) — Television Infrared Observation Satellite — and the first use of the term "remote sensing."

---

### Q14. Why is specialized knowledge required for RS data analysis?
**Ans:** Because RS images have unusual scales, resolutions, unfamiliar overhead view, and use regions of the EM spectrum beyond visible light.

---

### Q15. Name any four applications of Remote Sensing.
**Ans:** Environmental monitoring, flood/drought monitoring, urban planning, agricultural monitoring, forest fire detection, natural disaster management.

## Section B: From Lecture 2 (EMR and Remote Sensing)

---

### Q16. What is the most important source of EM energy at Earth's surface?
**Ans:** The **Sun**.

---

### Q17. What is the wavelength range of the Visible spectrum?
**Ans:** Approximately **0.38 µm to 0.7 µm**.

---

### Q18. Relate EMR with its interaction with Earth's surface.
**Ans:** When EMR reaches Earth's surface, three things happen: it is **reflected**, **absorbed**, or **transmitted**. The proportion of each depends on the **wavelength** of energy, the **material** type, and the **condition** of the surface feature.

---

### Q19. Why does Yellow color appear on an object in a remote sensing image?
**Ans:** Yellow appears when an object **absorbs Blue** light and **reflects Green + Red** light. The combination of Green and Red light produces Yellow.

---

### Q20. Why does Magenta color appear on an object in a remotely sensed image?
**Ans:** Magenta appears when an object **absorbs Green** light and **reflects Blue + Red** light. The combination of Blue and Red produces Magenta.

---

### Q21. What is Atmospheric Path Radiance?
**Ans:** It is scattered radiation within the atmosphere that reaches the sensor **without ever contacting the Earth's surface**, adding unwanted brightness/haze to the image.

---

### Q22. What causes Atmospheric Path Radiance to occur?
**Ans:** It occurs when atmospheric **particles or gas molecules** scatter the electromagnetic radiation, redirecting it toward the sensor without it touching the ground.

---

### Q23. What are Atmospheric Windows?
**Ans:** Regions of the EM spectrum that are **relatively free from absorption and scattering**, allowing radiation to pass through the atmosphere with little or no loss.

---

### Q24. Name the three main atmospheric windows used in RS.
**Ans:**
1. Optical window: **0.4 – 2 µm**
2. Thermal IR window: **~3 – 5 µm**
3. Broad thermal window: **~8 – 14 µm**

---

### Q25. What are the three main atmospheric gases that absorb EM radiation?
**Ans:** Ozone (O₃), Carbon Dioxide (CO₂), and Water Vapour (H₂O).

---

### Q26. Absorption, reflection, and emitted light portions in remote sensing depend on which factors?
**Ans:** They depend on:
1. **Wavelength** of the energy
2. **Material** type of the feature
3. **Condition** of the feature

---

### Q27. What is the effect of atmospheric scattering on remotely sensed images?
**Ans:**
- Adds **haze** to the image
- Records brightness of the atmosphere along with the target
- **Reduces contrast** → decreases detectability of features

---

### Q28. What is the radiation range in which remotely sensed images are made?
**Ans:** Primarily from the **Visible (0.4 µm) through Infrared (up to ~14 µm)**, and also the **Microwave** region — wherever atmospheric windows exist.

---

### Q29. Name three types of atmospheric scattering.
**Ans:**
1. **Rayleigh Scattering** — very small particles, affects shorter wavelengths
2. **Mie Scattering** — particle size ≈ wavelength, affects longer wavelengths
3. **Nonselective Scattering** — very large particles, scatters all wavelengths equally

---

### Q30. Why does the sky appear blue?
**Ans:** Because of **Rayleigh scattering** — shorter wavelengths (blue) are scattered much more than longer wavelengths as sunlight passes through the atmosphere.

---

### Q31. What is the difference between Specular and Diffuse reflection?
**Ans:**
- **Specular**: Smooth surface reflects energy in a **single direction** (mirror-like)
- **Diffuse**: Rough surface reflects energy **uniformly in all directions**

---

### Q32. What is Attenuation (Extinction)?
**Ans:** The **weakening** of radiation returning to the sensor from the Earth's surface, caused by the combined effects of atmospheric scattering and absorption.

---

### Q33. What is the difference between Reflected IR and Thermal IR?
**Ans:**
- **Reflected IR** (0.7–3.0 µm): Solar radiation **reflected** from Earth's surface
- **Thermal IR** (3.0–14.0 µm): Radiation **emitted** by the Earth as heat

---

### Q34. What is the color rule in Remote Sensing images?
**Ans:** The **color of an object** equals the **color of light it reflects**. It absorbs all other colors.

---

### Q35. What does White and Black represent in color mixing for RS images?
**Ans:**
- **White** = Object reflects **all three** (Blue + Green + Red)
- **Black** = Object absorbs **all** light, reflects none

---

## Section D: From Lecture 3 (Digital Image and Resolutions)

---

### Q41. What is a Pixel?
**Ans:** A pixel is the smallest discrete element of a digital image. It has both **spatial** (ground area size) and **spectral** (brightness intensity in a band) properties.

---

### Q42. What are the four types of resolution in Remote Sensing?
**Ans:** Spatial, Spectral, Radiometric, and Temporal.

---

### Q43. Define Spatial Resolution.
**Ans:** Spatial resolution is the size of the **smallest object** a sensor can detect, or the **ground area represented by one pixel**.

---

### Q44. What is the detection rule for spatial resolution?
**Ans:** To detect an object, the spatial resolution should be **less than half** the size of the object's smaller dimension. Example: 4 m object → need at least 2 m resolution.

---

### Q45. What is the difference between small scale and large scale maps?
**Ans:**
- **Small scale** (e.g., 1:100,000) = covers large area, less detail
- **Large scale** (e.g., 1:5,000) = covers small area, more detail

---

### Q46. Define Spectral Resolution.
**Ans:** Spectral resolution refers to the **number and width of wavelength bands** a sensor can record. Higher spectral resolution = narrower bandwidths.

---

### Q47. How many bands are needed for a color image?
**Ans:** At least **3 bands** (e.g., Red, Green, Blue) are needed for a color image.

---

### Q48. What is the difference between a Panchromatic and a Multispectral image?
**Ans:**
- **Panchromatic** = 1 band → gray scale image
- **Multispectral** = 3+ bands → color image

---

### Q49. Define Radiometric Resolution.
**Ans:** Radiometric resolution is the ability of a sensor to record **many levels of brightness**. The finer the radiometric resolution, the more sensitive it is to small energy differences.

---

### Q50. How many brightness levels (tones) can an 8-bit sensor record?
**Ans:** **256 tones** (values range from 0 to 255). Formula: $2^8 = 256$.

---

### Q51. What is the brightness value range for a 5-bit sensor?
**Ans:** **32 tones**, ranging from 0 to 31. Formula: $2^5 = 32$.

---

### Q52. Define Temporal Resolution.
**Ans:** Temporal resolution is how often a given place on Earth is **revisited and imaged** by a remote sensing system.

---

### Q53. What factors affect temporal resolution?
**Ans:** Satellite/sensor capabilities, swath overlap, and latitude.

---

### Q54. Which resolution is more important for urban land cover — spatial or spectral?
**Ans:** **Spatial resolution** is considered more important than spectral resolution for extracting urban land cover information.

---

### Q55. An object is 6 meters wide. What is the minimum spatial resolution needed to identify it?
**Ans:** **3 meters** (spatial resolution should be less than half the object's smaller dimension: 6/2 = 3 m).

---

### Q56. What is the formula to calculate the number of brightness levels from bit depth?
**Ans:** Number of tones = $2^n$, where **n** is the number of bits.

---

### Q57. What does a coarse spatial resolution mean?
**Ans:** Only **large features** are visible. Pixel size is typically **> 30 meters to > 1000 meters**.

---

## Section F: From Lecture 5 (RS Sensors and Data Acquisition)

---

### Q71. What is the difference between Multispectral and Hyperspectral RS?
**Ans:**
- **Multispectral** = collects data in **multiple (few) bands**
- **Hyperspectral** = collects data in **hundreds of bands**

---

### Q72. What are the two main types of remote sensors?
**Ans:** Photographic cameras (analog and digital) and Electro-Mechanical Scanners.

---

### Q73. What is a Scan Line?
**Ans:** A **horizontal row of pixels** in an image. It is collected either sequentially (whisk broom) or simultaneously (push broom).

---

### Q74. What is the difference between Whisk Broom and Push Broom scanners?
**Ans:**
- **Whisk Broom** (Across Track): Uses an **oscillating mirror**, images **one point** at a time
- **Push Broom** (Along Track): Uses a **linear CCD array**, images an **entire scan line** at once

---

### Q75. What is Dwell Time?
**Ans:** The time the **Instantaneous Field of View (IFOV)** "sees" a ground area. It is generally very short and influences sensor resolution design.

---

### Q76. Explain the trade-off between spatial, spectral, and radiometric resolutions.
**Ans:**
- **High spatial resolution** (small IFOV) → less energy detected → **reduced radiometric resolution**
- To improve radiometric resolution without losing spatial → must broaden wavelength range → **reduced spectral resolution**
- **Coarser spatial** resolution allows improved radiometric and/or spectral resolution
- All three must be **balanced** based on sensor objectives.

---

### Q77. How is satellite data transmitted to the ground?
**Ans:** Three ways:
1. **Direct transmission** — when satellite and ground station are in line of sight
2. **On-board recording** — data stored and transmitted later
3. **Relay via TDRSS** — data relayed satellite-to-satellite via communications satellites in geosynchronous orbit

---

### Q78. What is TDRSS?
**Ans:** **Tracking and Data Relay Satellite System** — a series of communications satellites in geosynchronous orbit used to relay data from observation satellites to ground stations.

---

### Q79. What is IFOV?
**Ans:** **Instantaneous Field of View** — the ground area seen by a sensor at any one instant of time.

---

### Q80. What is a CCD?
**Ans:** **Charged Couple Device** — a light-sensitive detector used in push broom scanners to capture an entire line of pixels simultaneously.

---

### Q81. What is the difference between Framing and Scanning systems?
**Ans:**
- **Framing System**: Captures the **entire scene at once** (like a camera)
- **Scanning System**: Builds the image **line by line**

---

### Q82. How is aircraft data retrieved compared to satellite data?
**Ans:**
- **Aircraft**: Data retrieved **when aircraft lands**, then processed
- **Satellite**: Data transmitted **electronically** to ground station (direct, recorded, or relayed)

---

## Section H: Satellite Orbits (Lecture 6)

### Q89. What is a geostationary orbit?
**Ans:** A satellite orbits at ~36,000 km altitude, matching Earth's rotation speed, so it stays fixed over one spot on the equator.

### Q90. What altitude do geostationary satellites orbit at?
**Ans:** Approximately **36,000 km** above Earth.

### Q91. What is the main use of geostationary satellites?
**Ans:** **Weather monitoring** — they provide continuous coverage of the same area.

### Q92. What is a polar orbit?
**Ans:** A satellite orbits from pole to pole (north-south), passing over different parts of Earth as it rotates underneath.

### Q93. What altitude do polar orbiting satellites typically fly at?
**Ans:** Approximately **700–1,000 km** above Earth.

### Q94. What is a sun-synchronous orbit?
**Ans:** A special polar orbit where the satellite passes over any given point at the **same local time** every revisit — ensuring consistent sun illumination.

### Q95. Why is consistent sun illumination important in remote sensing?
**Ans:** It ensures images taken on different dates have **similar lighting conditions**, making comparison easier.

### Q96. What is mid-morning crossing time and why is it preferred?
**Ans:** Around **10:00–10:30 AM local time**. Preferred because there is good sunlight with minimal cloud cover and shadows are not too long.

### Q97. What is the difference between ascending and descending passes?
**Ans:** **Ascending** = satellite moves south to north (nighttime). **Descending** = satellite moves north to south (daytime — when images are usually captured).

### Q98. What is swath width?
**Ans:** The **strip of ground** that a satellite sensor images in one pass. Wider swath = more area covered per orbit.

### Q99. What is revisit period?
**Ans:** The time it takes for a satellite to return and image the **same area** again (e.g., Landsat = 16 days).

### Q100. How does swath width affect revisit period?
**Ans:** **Wider swath** = shorter revisit period (covers more area per pass → returns sooner). **Narrow swath** = longer revisit.

## Section I: RS Data Formats (Lecture 7)

### Q101. Name the three digital image data storage schemes.
**Ans:** **BIP** (Band Interleaved by Pixel), **BIL** (Band Interleaved by Line), **BSQ** (Band Sequential).

### Q102. How does BIP store data?
**Ans:** All band values of **one pixel** are stored together before moving to the next pixel.

### Q103. How does BIL store data?
**Ans:** All bands of **one line** are stored together before moving to the next line.

### Q104. How does BSQ store data?
**Ans:** The **entire image for one band** is stored first, then the entire image for the next band.

### Q105. Draw/diagram how 3 bands are stored in BIP format.
**Ans:** Pixel1-B1, Pixel1-B2, Pixel1-B3, Pixel2-B1, Pixel2-B2, Pixel2-B3, ... (all bands of each pixel grouped together sequentially).

### Q106. What is the brightness value range for unsigned 8-bit data?
**Ans:** **0 to 255** (256 levels).

### Q107. What is the brightness value range for unsigned 16-bit data?
**Ans:** **0 to 65,535**.

### Q108. What is the brightness value range for signed 8-bit data?
**Ans:** **-128 to 127**.

### Q109. What is the unassigned bit range brightness value in 8 bits?
**Ans:** In signed 8-bit, the **negative values (-128 to -1)** are not used for brightness — they are the unassigned range.

### Q110. What is the difference between integer and floating point storage?
**Ans:** **Integer** stores whole numbers (e.g., 0–255); **floating point** stores decimal numbers with a very wide range, used for continuous data like elevation.

### Q111. Name one advantage of digital images over analog images.
**Ans:** No information loss during storage or copying, and no need for manual digitization.

### Q112. How many shades can a grayscale 8-bit image display?
**Ans:** **256 shades** of gray (0 = black, 255 = white).

## Section J: Digital Image & Digitization (Lecture 8)

### Q113. What are the two steps to convert an analog image to digital?
**Ans:** **Sampling** (dividing into a pixel grid) and **Quantization** (assigning a brightness number to each pixel).

### Q114. What is sampling in digitization?
**Ans:** Dividing the continuous scene into a **grid of pixels** — determines spatial resolution.

### Q115. What is quantization in digitization?
**Ans:** Assigning a **numeric brightness value** to each pixel — determines radiometric resolution.

### Q116. What does BV(i, j, k) represent?
**Ans:** **Brightness Value** at row i, column j, band k.

### Q117. What is metadata in a remote sensing image?
**Ans:** Descriptive information **about the image** — file name, date, rows, columns, bands, pixel size, georeferencing, statistics, quantization level.

### Q118. Give three examples of metadata items.
**Ans:** Number of rows, number of columns, and pixel size (or date of acquisition, number of bands, georeferencing — any three).

### Q119. What is a pixel neighborhood?
**Ans:** The **4 or 8 pixels** immediately surrounding a given pixel (4-connected = N,S,E,W; 8-connected = all 8 surrounding).

### Q120. What is the advantage of digital images over analog images?
**Ans:** No degradation over time, perfect copies, easier computer processing, can store more than 3 spectral bands.

### Q121. What is auxiliary data?
**Ans:** Additional supporting information stored alongside the image, such as map projection, sun elevation angle, and cloud cover percentage.

## Section K: Image Display, Subset & Mosaicking (Lecture 9)

### Q122. What is image subsetting?
**Ans:** **Cutting out a smaller area** from a larger image to save storage and focus on the study area.

### Q123. What is image mosaicking?
**Ans:** **Stitching multiple overlapping images** into one seamless large image.

### Q124. What are the requirements for mosaicking?
**Ans:** Same map projection, same datum, same pixel size, 20–30% overlap, and a designated base image.

### Q125. What is a base image in mosaicking?
**Ans:** The **reference image** to which all other images are aligned during the stitching process.

### Q126. What band combination produces a true color composite?
**Ans:** **RGB = 3, 2, 1** (Red band → R, Green band → G, Blue band → B).

### Q127. What band combination produces a false color composite?
**Ans:** **RGB = 4, 3, 2** (NIR → R, Red → G, Green → B).

### Q128. Why does vegetation appear red in a false color composite?
**Ans:** Vegetation reflects strongly in **NIR (Band 4)**, and NIR is assigned to the **Red color gun**, so vegetation appears bright red.

### Q129. What is a band composite?
**Ans:** A color image created by assigning different spectral bands to the Red, Green, and Blue color guns of a monitor.

### Q130. What is the SWIR composite band combination?
**Ans:** **RGB = 7, 4, 2** — useful for geology, soil, and moisture studies.

### Q131. How does healthy vegetation appear in true color vs false color?
**Ans:** **True color (3,2,1):** green. **False color (4,3,2):** bright red.

### Q132. How does water appear in false color composite?
**Ans:** **Dark blue or black** (water absorbs NIR, so the Red gun gets low values).

## Section L: Univariate Statistics (Lecture 10)

### Q133. What is a histogram in remote sensing?
**Ans:** A bar chart showing the **frequency of each brightness value** in an image band.

### Q134. What does a narrow/clustered histogram indicate?
**Ans:** **Low contrast** — the image appears dull or flat with little variation.

### Q135. What does a wide/spread histogram indicate?
**Ans:** **High contrast** — the image has good variation and appears sharp.

### Q136. Define mean in image statistics.
**Ans:** The **average brightness value** = sum of all pixel values ÷ number of pixels.

### Q137. Define median.
**Ans:** The **middle value** when all pixel values are arranged in ascending order.

### Q138. Define mode.
**Ans:** The **most frequently occurring** brightness value in the dataset.

### Q139. What is the formula for variance?
**Ans:** **Variance (σ²) = Σ(xi − mean)² / n** — average of squared deviations from the mean.

### Q140. What is standard deviation?
**Ans:** The **square root of variance** (σ = √σ²). Measures spread of values in the same units as the data.

### Q141. What does a large standard deviation indicate?
**Ans:** Pixel values are **spread far from the mean** — high variation in brightness.

### Q142. What is the difference between population and sample?
**Ans:** **Population** = complete set of all values. **Sample** = a subset taken from the population for analysis.

### Q143. What is a normal distribution?
**Ans:** A **bell-shaped curve** where most values cluster near the mean, with fewer values toward the extremes.

### Q144. What is range in statistics?
**Ans:** **Maximum value − Minimum value** — simplest measure of data spread.

### Q145. Why do we assess image quality using statistics?
**Ans:** To detect **errors, noise, or sensor problems** before using the image for analysis.

## Section M: Multivariate Statistics (Lecture 11)

### Q146. What is the difference between univariate and multivariate statistics?
**Ans:** **Univariate** analyzes one band at a time. **Multivariate** analyzes the relationship between **two or more bands** together.

### Q147. What does covariance measure?
**Ans:** How **two bands change together** — whether they increase/decrease in the same or opposite directions.

### Q148. What does a positive covariance mean?
**Ans:** Both bands **increase together** — they have similar behavior.

### Q149. What does a negative covariance mean?
**Ans:** When one band increases, the other **decreases** — opposite behavior.

### Q150. What is the correlation coefficient?
**Ans:** A **standardized covariance** value that ranges from **-1 to +1**, making it easier to compare relationships between bands.

### Q151. What is the formula for correlation coefficient?
**Ans:** **r_kl = cov_kl / (σ_k × σ_l)** — covariance divided by the product of standard deviations of the two bands.

### Q152. What does r = +1 mean?
**Ans:** **Perfect positive correlation** — both bands behave exactly the same way.

### Q153. What does r = 0 mean?
**Ans:** **No correlation** — the two bands are completely independent of each other.

### Q154. What does r = -1 mean?
**Ans:** **Perfect negative correlation** — bands behave in exactly opposite ways.

### Q155. If two bands have high correlation (r > 0.9), what does it mean?
**Ans:** They carry **similar/redundant information** — you could drop one without losing much data.

### Q156. What is a variance-covariance matrix?
**Ans:** A table where the **diagonal** shows variance of each band and the **off-diagonal** shows covariance between each pair of bands.

### Q157. What is always on the diagonal of a correlation matrix?
**Ans:** **1.00** — because every band is perfectly correlated with itself.

### Q158. What is SP (Sum of Products)?
**Ans:** The sum of multiplied deviations of two bands: **SP_kl = Σ(BV_ik − mean_k)(BV_il − mean_l)**. Covariance = SP/n.

### Q159. Why is correlation preferred over covariance for comparison?
**Ans:** Correlation is **standardized (-1 to +1)**, so you can compare relationships between any pair of bands regardless of their units or value ranges.

### Q160. How do you use correlation matrix to reduce data size?
**Ans:** Find bands with **high correlation** (r > 0.9) — they are redundant. You can **remove one** from each highly correlated pair to reduce data without losing much information.
